⚡ Bitcoin's electricity in numbers

Here's a balanced snapshot from the Cambridge Digital Mining Industry Report, published April 2025. These are historical estimates, not live measurements.

0.54%

42.6%

Renewable electricity reported by surveyed miners (wind, solar, hydro and more).

47.6%

Fossil-fuel electricity reported by surveyed miners (mainly natural gas and coal).

Important: Cambridge surveyed 49 firms representing around 48% of Bitcoin's mining computing power. Their reported energy mix is not a full global census. Electricity use and greenhouse-gas emissions are modelled network-wide estimates. Read Cambridge's methodology and findings ↗

🌬️ Where does mining's power come from?

According to the Cambridge 2025 survey, just over half the reported electricity came from renewable and nuclear sources combined. Nuclear is low-carbon, but it is not renewable.

🌱 The good, the bad & the complicated

🌱 The good

Mining can use renewable electricity, make use of electricity that would otherwise be curtailed, and switch off quickly when the grid needs power. Cambridge recorded miners reporting 888 GWh of curtailed load in 2023. These benefits depend on the location and arrangement.

🏭 The bad

Mining still consumes a great deal of electricity, some powered by fossil fuels. Cambridge's survey-based estimate was about 39.8 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions annually; a different modelling approach produced a much higher estimate. Mining hardware also creates electronic waste.

⚖️ The complicated

Using a renewable source does not automatically make mining harmless. Electricity used by a miner might otherwise have served a household or displaced fossil-fuel generation. The real impact depends on where and when electricity is consumed — and what would have happened without mining.

🔮 Greener tomorrow? What might power Bitcoin next?

There is no trustworthy single forecast for Bitcoin's renewable-energy share in 2030. Here's what could change — and what could hold it back. These are possibilities, not predictions.

☀️ More wind & solar?

Miners may move towards cheaper renewable power and operate mainly when plentiful energy is available. But mining needs to be genuinely flexible: electricity called “green” isn't automatically spare electricity.

🔌 A flexible grid customer?

Mining can shut down quickly during grid stress. Cambridge's surveyed miners reported 888 GWh of curtailed load during 2023. That's evidence of flexibility, not proof that every operation makes a grid greener.

♨️ Waste heat & gas?

Some projects seek to reuse mining heat, or run on gas that would otherwise be flared. The emissions benefit depends on what would otherwise happen — burning gas can still create CO₂, and methane leakage matters.

Three possible futures — not numerical forecasts

🌱 Greener route

More truly surplus renewables, cleaner power grids, demand response and useful heat recovery could reduce emissions per unit of electricity used.

🌧️ Dirtier route

Rising mining demand, fossil-powered expansion and weak oversight could push absolute electricity use and emissions higher, even as machines become more efficient.

A key twist: Cambridge found mining hardware efficiency improved around 24% year-on-year by June 2024, while annualised network electricity consumption still rose around 17%. More efficient machines don't guarantee less total electricity use. Cambridge report ↗

🚗 What does the EV comparison tell us?

The IEA estimates the worldwide electric-vehicle fleet used about 180 TWh of electricity in 2024, roughly 30% more than Cambridge's 138 TWh annualised Bitcoin mining estimate. EV charging replaces some petrol and diesel use, so electricity demand alone does not measure overall climate impact. IEA source ↗

What about social media, Netflix or AI?

There is no similarly robust, up-to-date global electricity figure for all social media alone in these sources. Social media, video streaming and AI share data-centre infrastructure, and users' phones, televisions, networks and home routers also consume electricity. Comparing a social-media estimate with the whole Bitcoin network without matching boundaries would be misleading.

One useful comparison: Bitcoin's 138 TWh annualised estimate was roughly one-third of the IEA's 415 TWh for all data centres in 2024. That's a comparison of scale, not a ranking of usefulness, carbon emissions or environmental harm.

🌍 Where is Bitcoin mining greenest?

These five countries have promising low-carbon electricity for mining — this is not a verified ranking of individual mining operations. Clean grid power is not necessarily surplus power.

🇮🇸 Iceland

Hydro & geothermal: Almost all electricity is renewable, and cool weather reduces cooling needs. Catch: limited grid capacity and competing demand.

🇳🇴 Norway

Hydro & wind: A predominantly low-carbon grid and cool climate. Catch: power has other valuable uses, and mining expansion faces policy scrutiny.

🇵🇾 Paraguay

Hydropower: Electricity from projects such as Itaipu attracts miners. Catch: power could instead serve local industry or be exported.

🇧🇹 Bhutan

Hydropower: State-linked Bitcoin mining uses Bhutan's hydro resources. Catch: generation varies seasonally; other consumers need priority.

🇪🇹 Ethiopia

Hydropower: New hydro generation has attracted miners. Catch: reliable electricity access for households and businesses remains a major priority.

🍻 A pub fact

A Bitcoin mined with Icelandic geothermal power and one mined with coal are identical Bitcoins — but their environmental footprints can differ dramatically.

💬 Questions your friends might ask

Does Bitcoin really use more electricity than some countries?

Yes. Cambridge estimated around 138 terawatt-hours each year, comparable with the annual electricity use of some countries. Comparisons show the scale, but not whether the electricity comes from clean or polluting sources.

Is Bitcoin mostly powered by renewables?

Not in the 2025 Cambridge survey: renewables were 42.6%. The often-quoted 52.4% figure includes 9.8% nuclear, which is low-carbon but not renewable. The survey represented roughly 48% of mining computing power, not every miner worldwide.

Can Bitcoin mining help wind and solar farms?

Potentially. Flexible demand can buy otherwise wasted renewable electricity or help some projects earn revenue. But it doesn't automatically reduce emissions: in some places it might divert cleaner electricity away from other users.

Does Bitcoin produce electronic waste?

Yes. Mining machines become obsolete. Cambridge's report estimated approximately 2.3 kilotonnes of mining-related electronic waste in 2024, while noting resale, reuse and recycling of decommissioned machines.

Is Bitcoin getting greener?

Some surveyed miners reported cleaner power mixes and more efficient equipment, but estimated total electricity consumption has also risen. Improving efficiency alone doesn't guarantee a smaller overall environmental footprint.

🍻 Five pub facts

  1. Bitcoin uses electricity: roughly 0.54% of global consumption in Cambridge's 2025 estimate.
  2. Renewable is not the same as low-carbon: 42.6% renewables, or 52.4% including nuclear, among surveyed miners.
  3. Fossil fuels still matter: natural gas was the largest single reported energy source at 38.2%.
  4. Mining can switch off: some operators cut demand when grids are under pressure.
  5. It's not an all-or-nothing story: the net impact depends on the source, location and alternatives for that electricity.

These are short conversation starters rather than an environmental verdict. All figures reflect research published in April 2025.

So, is Bitcoin green?

Not entirely — but not entirely dirty either. Bitcoin uses considerable electricity, and much of it still comes from fossil fuels. Some mining makes use of cleaner, flexible energy; other mining adds pollution or competes with other electricity users. A fair answer weighs where the power comes from, what it might otherwise have powered, and whether the benefits justify the costs.
